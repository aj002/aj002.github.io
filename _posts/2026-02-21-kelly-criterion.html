Kelly sizing when your probability is wrong

What a five-point error in the win probability does to a Kelly-sized bet.

Suppose a coin pays even money and lands heads 60% of the time. Kelly gives a bet of 20% of the current bankroll. Now suppose 60% was an estimate, and the true probability is 55%. The growth-optimal bet is only 10%. That five-point error has doubled the stake.

The calculation is short enough to check directly.

The bet

Let f be the fraction of the bankroll staked on heads. A win multiplies wealth by 1 + f; a loss multiplies it by 1 − f. Assume independent rounds with an unchanged win probability p, no fees, and a stake between zero and the whole bankroll.

Expected log growth per round
G(f; p) = p ln(1 + f) + (1 − p) ln(1 − f)

Logarithms let us add the changes across rounds. Under these assumptions, the average log return converges to G as the number of rounds grows. Differentiating with respect to f gives:

p / (1 + f) − (1 − p) / (1 − f) = 0
So f* = 2p − 1. If p ≤ 0.5, the best permitted stake on heads is zero.

With known p = 0.60, the optimum is f = 0.20. Ten per cent is half Kelly. Forty per cent is double Kelly.

Change the probability, keep the stake

Each entry below is expected log growth per round, using natural logarithms. The stake stays fixed while the true win probability changes.

True p10% stake20% stake40% stake
60%0.0150420.020136−0.002447
55%0.005008−0.000138−0.044812
50%−0.005025−0.020411−0.087177

At 60%, the 20% stake has the highest growth of the three. At 55%, it has slightly negative expected log growth, despite the coin still favouring heads. The 10% stake is now the optimum. At 50%, every positive stake in the table has negative log growth.

Also: double Kelly does not give exactly zero growth here. At a 60% win probability and a 40% stake, G = −0.002447. The zero-at-double-Kelly rule comes from a quadratic approximation; it is not an exact result for this coin.

How much accuracy does the bet need?

For a fixed stake, we can solve G(f; p) = 0 for the break-even win probability:

pbreak-even = −ln(1 − f) / [ln(1 + f) − ln(1 − f)]

A 20% stake needs a true win probability above about 55.034% for positive expected log growth. A 10% stake needs about 52.504%. Halving the stake lowers that threshold by about 2.5 percentage points.

These are statements about repeated log growth. They do not promise a profit over a fixed number of rounds. A strategy with positive expected log growth can still have a bad run, and a coin with no edge still has negative expected log growth at every positive stake.

Before using a Kelly fraction, I’d want to see this calculation over a range of plausible probabilities. A single estimate of p hides how close the chosen stake is to negative growth.

Source and calculation

J. L. Kelly Jr., A New Interpretation of Information Rate (1956). The table above is a direct evaluation of the even-money formula, not a backtest.

from math import log1p

def growth(p, f):
    return p * log1p(f) + (1 - p) * log1p(-f)

for p in (0.60, 0.55, 0.50):
    print(p, [round(growth(p, f), 6) for f in (0.10, 0.20, 0.40)])
